The AI Showrunner — Director’s Brief

Your Next VFX Shot Shouldn’t Start With a Six-Week Pipeline

By Jeff Jones · October 30, 2026

A dragon clears a waterfall. A rider holds on. Water, atmosphere, scale, and camera movement either sell the moment—or expose the machinery.

The audience experiences a shot. The producer pays for the process behind it.

That distinction matters when comparing conventional visual effects with generative production. Modeling, surfacing, rigging, animation, simulation, lighting, rendering, and compositing can deliver extraordinary control. They can also commit substantial resources before a director knows whether the scene belongs in the film.

AI-first development changes when that commitment becomes necessary.

What the traditional VFX pipeline builds

A conventional creature shot can involve a chain of specialized assets and departments. The reference below maps the common path from a 3D model through rigging and skinning, animation, lighting and rendering, and compositing. The exact handoffs vary by production, but each stage adds control—and potential revision work.

Character transformation: build the change or explore it

A CG character can absolutely transform. For a human-to-werewolf change, a traditional pipeline may use alternate sculpts, blendshapes, rig adjustments, simulation, and carefully animated transitions. That can deliver precise, repeatable control, while adding asset and shot work.

With AI-assisted production, approved character sheets and visual references can guide rapid transformation explorations across frames or shots. The result still needs review for identity continuity, anatomy, motion, and editorial control. The two approaches offer different control points and tradeoffs.
