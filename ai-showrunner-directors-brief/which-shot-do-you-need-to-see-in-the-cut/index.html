The AI Showrunner — Director’s Brief

Which Shot Do You Need to See in the Cut?

By Jeff Jones · November 18, 2026

Picture a five-second push toward a coastal signal station. The building is dark except for one amber window. In the first cut, the rain arrives before your eye finds the light. The director has one note. “Give me another second to find that window. Keep the camera move.”

That is the moment I want a producer to judge Storyforge. An impressive first frame tells you something about taste. The second pass tells you whether you can direct us.

The opportunity from both sides

Storyforge brings a director-led workflow, original scenes and experience finishing a film against a 48-hour festival deadline. The weakness I want the test to expose is whether a precise client revision can hold its approved details without more cleanup than the producer expected. One moving shot, with a time log and a second pass, is a fair place to learn that.

For the producer, the opportunity is to see an option before committing a larger team. The concern is practical: who approved the input, where did it go, and can the exterior remain consistent after a note? We agree on permitted tools and cleared references before any project material is shared. We show remaining work alongside the result. A private review can answer those questions without putting a show or its crew on public display.

What a first look can buy

A producer might have a review on Thursday and a full environment build penciled in for later. The question for Thursday could be as small as whether this exterior earns five seconds before the cut inside. If our studio can put a moving concept in the edit within hours of brief approval, the director can try the transition while there is still time to change it. The answer might even save the team from commissioning a shot it would eventually cut.

Hours to a first look is a target to test on a suitable brief. A finished effects shot has its own schedule. On Operation Dumpster Thunder, I worked from a Friday-afternoon festival brief to a Sunday-afternoon 4K render. That gives a producer a piece of my work to watch and a deadline to put it in context. A client shot with exact match requirements deserves its own test. Watch the film and read my production notes.

Where the work goes

For a conventional CG version, the supervisor might begin with an existing building asset. A modeler could change the roof mesh, a look-development artist could tune how wet metal catches light, and a lighting artist could isolate the glow from the window. Layout sets the camera. Effects and compositing artists can change the onset of rain independently. A rig would matter if a mechanical part needed to articulate. On a show that already has the set and camera, the fastest way to take the note might be to move an existing rain element in comp.

For an AI-first version, I would start with a cleared reference and get agreement on the station’s outline, the camera path and the moment the light must register. I would generate motion tests, bring the strongest take into the cut, then inspect it frame by frame. If a generation delays the storm but moves the window, I would try keeping the accepted building plate and controlling the rain in comp. If production needs that same building from eight more angles, the reusable CG asset belongs in the bid conversation.

Match the production’s approved look

A production may specify approved camera bodies and recording modes, lens sets, a frame rate, and a delivery raster before the first shot. The cinematographer also makes choices about depth of field, camera movement, lighting and palette that carry from one scene to the next. Those choices belong in our brief. I use them to guide the generated shot and judge how it plays beside the surrounding footage.

Give me the camera or capture device, sensor mode or crop, lens series and focal length, and the intended aperture and focus falloff. Tell me whether the shot should look spherical or anamorphic, how the camera should move, and what motion blur, grain, flare or distortion should carry through. An approved graded still or adjacent shot helps establish contrast, skin tones, shadow color and highlight roll-off. If your colorist provides a LUT, identify its expected input and output and whether it is a viewing reference or part of the deliverable.

Generated footage can emulate the look of a named camera and lens. We evaluate that match against your references and keep capture provenance accurate. If a shot requires actual acquisition on an approved camera or specific camera-original files, flag that when we scope the test. We also need separate approval for the generation and post tools that will handle production material.

Before rendering, confirm the exact frame rate, raster, aspect ratio, framing guides and delivery format. Review the result against the adjacent shots at playback speed and inspect the cut for focus, motion blur, continuity and grade. Your director, DP or colorist can flag any visible mismatch before the shot moves forward.

Put the clips in the same timeline

Agree on duration, frame rate, resolution, starting assets and the standard for an editorial review clip. Give each available route the same brief, the same note and the same viewing conditions. An existing internal version can be the baseline if it was made to comparable requirements. A producer can also bring a shelved idea and evaluate the Storyforge version on its own. Any estimate of what another route might have taken stays labeled as an estimate.

Record elapsed hours to the first reviewable clip and to the revision. Track hands-on hours, waiting, generation or render costs and cleanup separately. Then ask the editor to play both versions between the adjoining shots. Does the eye land where the director intended? Does the roofline stay put? Can the note be addressed without reopening an approved part of the frame? Record which version the editor would use, and what still needs finishing. A fast first look that creates two extra days of repair belongs in the results.

Bring us one shot

Storyforge Imaginarium is inviting producers, post teams and production partners to propose one five-second shot for a studio-funded case study. A shelved environment, a teaser reveal or an alternate transition can work. For a selected brief, we provide an initial moving concept, one revision, both review clips and a time log at no cost. Your team can review privately and decide whether to request a separate production bid. We agree on approved tools and inputs before receiving project materials. Publishing a partner’s work or naming a production requires its written approval.
