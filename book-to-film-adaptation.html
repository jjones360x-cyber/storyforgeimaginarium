Book-to-screen development

How to Adapt a Book into a Movie Script

By Jeff Jones · Storyforge Imaginarium · October 28, 2026

A novel can spend pages inside a character’s thoughts. A film needs decisions, behavior, images, and sound. Adapting a book into a screenplay starts by deciding what the audience must experience—not by copying chapters into screenplay software.

1. Choose the story the film will tell

Write one sentence naming the protagonist, the goal, the opposing force, and the cost of failure. Then identify the relationship or internal conflict that makes that goal matter. This becomes the test for every subplot: does it strengthen the central journey, or compete with it?

For a hypothetical novel about an archivist who discovers that a city erases its own history, the dramatic spine might be her attempt to preserve evidence before the next erasure. A chapter about the city’s ancient founders may inform the world bible without becoming an entire scene.

2. Decide between a feature and a series

A feature needs a satisfying central arc within one film. A series needs a repeatable source of conflict that can sustain episodes. A long book is not automatically a television series. Ask what changes each episode, what remains unresolved, and why viewers would return after the initial mystery is answered.

3. Build an adaptation map before drafting

Create a working table with five columns: source passage, dramatic purpose, proposed scene, adaptation change, and approval status. Mark essential moments, characters that could be combined, and exposition that can become action. Keep a separate list of the author’s non-negotiables.

When combining characters, preserve the function that matters. A mentor and a sibling may both challenge the hero, but collapsing them can remove a crucial emotional relationship. Record the tradeoff rather than treating compression as a purely mechanical exercise.

4. Write the treatment, then the scenes

A treatment describes the screen story in prose, including the ending. A scene outline then identifies who wants what, what stands in the way, and what changes. Draft the screenplay after those decisions are approved. Dialogue should emerge from conflict and character, with visual action carrying information wherever possible.

For our archivist, “she distrusts the council” is an internal statement. Her quietly photographing a document before surrendering it turns that distrust into visible behavior. The screenplay gains a playable action and a future consequence.

5. Review against both the book and the screen experience

Use two passes. In the fidelity pass, check motivations, chronology, relationships, and the source’s central promise. In the screen pass, check pacing, redundant scenes, unclear objectives, and production scope. A change can depart from a chapter while preserving its emotional purpose; explain that choice to the rights holder.

What a useful first commission looks like

Start with a paid adaptation assessment and treatment. Agree on the format, source material, deliverables, review milestones, and revision allowance. A full feature screenplay or pilot can follow as a separate phase. A cinematic concept trailer can help communicate tone, but it does not replace a script.

Before development begins, establish who can authorize the adaptation and how the materials may be used. Storyforge works with authors, publishers, producers, and authorized rights holders; a bestselling title is not itself permission to adapt it.
